Understanding TDEE

The master number behind every successful diet plan.

🧮 How Is TDEE Calculated?

TDEE = BMR × Activity Factor

Your Total Daily Energy Expenditure is built in two steps. First, we calculate your BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) — the calories your body burns at complete rest — using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics considers the most accurate formula for most people:

  • Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
  • Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Then we multiply your BMR by an activity factor that reflects your lifestyle:

Activity LevelMultiplierWho Is This?
Sedentary× 1.2Desk job, little or no exercise
Light× 1.375Light exercise 1–3 days per week
Moderate× 1.55Exercise 3–5 days per week
Active× 1.725Hard exercise 6–7 days per week
Very Active× 1.9Physical job plus daily training

⚖️ TDEE vs BMR: What's the Difference?

Many people confuse these two numbers, but they serve very different purposes:

  • BMR is the energy your body needs just to stay alive — breathing, circulating blood, maintaining body temperature, and keeping your organs working. If you stayed in bed all day without moving, you'd still burn your BMR.
  • TDEE is your BMR plus everything else: walking to the kitchen, working at your desk, exercising, and even digesting your meals.

This is why you should never eat below your BMR for extended periods — it's the minimum fuel your body needs to function properly. Your TDEE, on the other hand, is the number you base your diet plan on.

🎯 How to Use Your TDEE for Any Goal

Once you know your TDEE, reaching any body goal becomes simple math:

  • Fat loss: Eat 400–500 kcal below your TDEE. This creates a sustainable deficit that leads to roughly 0.5 kg of fat loss per week — fast enough to see progress, slow enough to keep your muscle.
  • Maintenance: Eat at your TDEE. Your weight stays stable because the energy going in matches the energy going out.
  • Muscle gain: Eat 250–300 kcal above your TDEE. A small surplus fuels muscle growth without adding unnecessary fat. Bigger surpluses mostly just add fat.

Important: These are starting points, not guarantees. Track your weight for 2–3 weeks and adjust by 100–200 kcal if you're not seeing the expected change.

🔥 What Makes Up Your TDEE?

Your daily calorie burn comes from four sources:

  • BMR (60–75%): The biggest chunk — just keeping you alive. This is why even sedentary people burn significant calories.
  • NEAT (15–20%): Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis — all the movement that isn't formal exercise: walking, fidgeting, cleaning, standing. This varies hugely between people and is why some seem to "eat anything without gaining weight."
  • Exercise (5–15%): Your workouts. Surprisingly, this is often the smallest piece — an hour at the gym burns less than most people think.
  • TEF (~10%): The Thermic Effect of Food — the energy used to digest and process what you eat. Protein has the highest TEF, which is one reason high-protein diets help with fat loss.

❌ Common TDEE Mistakes to Avoid

  • Overestimating activity: Most people should pick one level lower than they think. Three gym sessions a week is "Moderate," not "Active."
  • Cutting calories too aggressively: Eating 1,000+ kcal below your TDEE causes muscle loss, fatigue, and rebound weight gain. Slow and steady wins.
  • Never recalculating: As you lose weight, your TDEE drops — a lighter body burns fewer calories. Recalculate every 4–6 weeks or after every 5 kg of change.
  • Trusting fitness trackers blindly: Watches and machines often overestimate calorie burn by 20–50%. Your TDEE calculation is usually more reliable.

🔄 When Should You Recalculate?

Your TDEE isn't fixed — it changes as your body changes. Recalculate when:

  • Your weight changes by 5 kg or more (in either direction)
  • Your activity level changes (new job, new workout routine, injury)
  • Your progress stalls for 2–3 weeks despite consistent eating
  • Every 4–6 weeks during an active diet phase, as a general check-in

Remember: any calculator gives an estimate. Your real-world results over 2–3 weeks are the ultimate truth — adjust your intake based on what the scale and mirror tell you.

FAQs

TDEE Questions, Answered

Quick answers to common TDEE questions.

What is TDEE?

TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) is the total number of calories your body burns in a day, including all activities — from breathing and digestion to exercise. It's calculated by multiplying your BMR by an activity factor.

How is TDEE calculated?

TDEE = BMR × Activity Factor. We first calculate your BMR using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, then multiply it by a factor (1.2 to 1.9) based on how active you are.

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR is the calories you burn at complete rest (just staying alive). TDEE includes everything — walking, working, exercising, and digesting food. TDEE is always higher than BMR.

How do I use my TDEE to lose weight?

Eat fewer calories than your TDEE. A deficit of 500 calories per day leads to roughly 0.5 kg of fat loss per week. To gain muscle, eat 250–300 calories above your TDEE.

Is my data saved when I use this calculator?

No. All calculations run entirely in your browser. Your details and results are never stored or sent anywhere.

Is my TDEE the same every day?

Not exactly. Your TDEE varies day to day based on how much you move. The calculator gives your average based on your typical activity level. On very active days you burn more; on rest days you burn less. This is why weekly averages matter more than any single day.

Why is my TDEE lower than I expected?

Most people overestimate their activity level. An hour at the gym doesn't fully offset 8+ hours of sitting. If your result seems low, try the "Light" or "Sedentary" level, eat to that target for 2–3 weeks, and adjust based on your actual results.
