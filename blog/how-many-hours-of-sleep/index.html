How Many Hours of Sleep Do You Need? (By Age Chart)

Most adults need 7 to 9 hours — but the number that matters is the one your body actually gets, night after night. Here's the recommended range by age, how 90-minute sleep cycles set your bedtime, and why short sleep quietly stalls fat loss and strength gains.

Ask ten people how much sleep they get and you'll hear ten different numbers, usually followed by "I'm fine on six." Ask them how they feel at 3pm and the story changes.

The short answer: most adults aged 18 to 64 need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Teens need 8 to 10, school-age kids 9 to 12, and adults over 65 need 7 to 8. Those numbers come from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Foundation, and they describe the amount linked to the best health outcomes across large populations — not a personal best.

This guide covers the full chart, why a range is more honest than one number, how to count backwards from your alarm using sleep cycles, and what consistently falling short does to your appetite, your training and your waistline.

How Many Hours of Sleep Do You Actually Need?

Seven to nine hours is the target band for healthy adults. Within that band, where you land depends on genetics, age, activity level, stress and how good your sleep quality is. Someone sleeping 7 hours of solid, uninterrupted sleep can be better recovered than someone in bed for 9 hours who wakes up four times.

What the research is clear about is the floor. Below about 7 hours, the risks climb steadily: higher body weight, worse blood sugar control, more injuries, lower training performance and a mood that gets flatter. Six hours a night is not "almost enough" — it's a chronic deficit of 7 to 21 hours a week.

Sleep Chart by Age

Total sleep per 24 hours, naps included. These are the ranges most sleep-medicine bodies agree on.

Age group Recommended sleep Notes
Newborns (0–3 months) 14–17 hours Split across day and night, no schedule yet
Babies (4–11 months) 12–16 hours Includes 2–3 naps
Toddlers (1–2 years) 11–14 hours Includes 1–2 naps
Preschoolers (3–5 years) 10–13 hours Usually one afternoon nap
School age (6–12 years) 9–12 hours Naps fade out; bedtime routine matters most
Teens (13–18 years) 8–10 hours Body clock naturally shifts later — early school starts fight it
Adults (18–64 years) 7–9 hours The band where most people land on 7.5
Older adults (65+ years) 7–8 hours Sleep gets lighter and more broken; total need barely drops

Two things surprise people here. First, older adults don't need dramatically less sleep — they get less, often because of lighter sleep and more night waking, not because their body needs 5 hours. Second, teenagers aren't lazy: puberty shifts the circadian clock later by up to two hours, so a 15-year-old genuinely cannot fall asleep at 9pm the way a 9-year-old can.

⚠️ If you consistently need 9+ hours and still wake up exhausted, that's not laziness and it isn't fixed by more coffee. Loud snoring, gasping, morning headaches or falling asleep during the day are reasons to see a doctor — sleep apnoea is common and treatable.

Sleep Cycles and Your Bedtime

Sleep isn't a flat block of unconsciousness. You move through cycles of roughly 90 minutes: light sleep, deep sleep, then REM sleep, then a brief near-waking moment before the next cycle.

  • Deep sleep dominates the first half of the night. This is where physical recovery and growth hormone release happen — the part that matters for training.
  • REM sleep dominates the second half. This is where memory, mood and learning get processed.

That's why cutting sleep from 8 hours to 6 doesn't just remove two random hours — it removes mostly REM, because you're truncating the back end of the night. It also explains the classic advice: count in 90-minute blocks, not round numbers.

Bedtime, working backwards
Bedtime = wake time − (cycles × 90 min) − 15 min
The extra 15 minutes is the average time it takes to fall asleep. Five cycles ≈ 7.5 hours in bed.

How to Tell If You're Sleeping Enough

Hours in bed are an input; how you feel and function is the output. Three quick checks:

  1. You wake up without needing an alarm most days — or at least wake up before it and feel human.
  2. You don't hit a wall at 3pm that only caffeine or sugar fixes.
  3. You fall asleep within 15–30 minutes of lying down. Instantly? You're probably sleep-deprived. Lying awake for an hour? Your bedtime is too early or your wind-down is broken.
Sign Sleeping enough Running a deficit
Morning Wake before the alarm, no dread Hit snooze 2–3 times, groggy for an hour
Afternoon Steady energy, no slump 3pm crash, cravings for sugar or coffee
Training Same weights feel the same Warm-ups feel heavy, motivation gone
Appetite Hunger predictable, meals satisfying Snacking, never quite full, more takeaway
Mood Patient, even-tempered Short fuse, low motivation, flat

If two or more of the right-hand column sound familiar for weeks at a time, you have your answer regardless of what your tracker says.

What Short Sleep Does to Fat Loss and the Gym

Sleep doesn't burn calories while you're asleep in any magical way. What it does is control the systems that decide whether your diet works. Three mechanisms explain most of it:

1. Hunger hormones move in the wrong direction

After short sleep, ghrelin (the hunger signal) rises and leptin (the fullness signal) falls. In controlled studies, people who slept about 4–5 hours ate roughly 250–350 extra calories the next day without noticing — mostly from snacks. That's enough to erase a 500-calorie deficit by half or more.

2. Glucose control gets worse

A single night of very short sleep can reduce insulin sensitivity noticeably. Do that for a week and your body handles carbohydrates like a less healthy version of itself — which is the opposite of what you want while dieting. The sleep and weight loss guide goes deeper into this loop.

3. Recovery and injury risk

Growth hormone release, muscle repair and skill learning all happen mostly in deep sleep and REM. Athletes sleeping under 8 hours report more injuries and worse performance. If you're chasing numbers like the ones in the strength standards charts, sleep is one of the variables you can actually control — and skipping it caps what your programme can deliver.

None of this means sleep is a fat-loss shortcut. It means a calorie deficit is much easier to hold when you're rested, and much harder to hold when you're tired, hungry and unmotivated. If your plan isn't moving, check whether your calorie target is right — and then check your sleep before assuming the diet failed.

Sleep Debt, Weekends and Naps

Sleep debt is real but only partly repayable. A few common beliefs, checked against what the evidence says:

Belief Reality
"I'll catch up at the weekend" A lie-in helps the sleepiness, but doesn't fully reverse the appetite and glucose effects — and it pushes your body clock later, making Monday worse
"Six hours is fine, I'm used to it" People adapt to the feeling of being tired, not to the impairment. Performance tests still show the deficit
"A nap makes up for a bad night" A short nap restores alertness; it doesn't replace REM or deep sleep lost overnight. Keep it under 30 minutes and before mid-afternoon
"Alcohol helps me fall asleep" It helps you pass out and then fragments the second half of the night, cutting REM. You wake unrefreshed
"I only need 5 hours, it's genetic" True short sleepers exist and are rare. If you need coffee to function, you're probably not one of them

Naps are a tool, not a substitute. 10–20 minutes restores alertness with no grogginess. 90 minutes gives you a full cycle if you badly need it. Anything in between — around 40–60 minutes — drops you into deep sleep and you wake up worse than before.

How to Fix Your Sleep in 7 Steps

Most sleep problems are habits, not mysteries. In rough order of impact:

1. Fix your wake time first

Same wake time seven days a week, including weekends — within about an hour. Your body clock anchors to the morning, not the bedtime. Getting up at the same time is what makes falling asleep at the same time possible.

2. Get light in your eyes within 30 minutes of waking

Ten minutes outdoors (or by a bright window) tells your brain the day has started and sets the timer for sleepiness roughly 14–16 hours later. This is the single most underrated sleep habit.

3. Cut caffeine after 2pm

Caffeine has a half-life of about 5–6 hours, so a 4pm coffee still has a quarter of its punch at 10pm. If you're sensitive, move the cutoff earlier and watch how much easier bedtime gets.

4. Keep the room cool and dark

Around 18–19°C with blackout conditions works for most people. Your core temperature has to drop to initiate deep sleep, and a warm room blocks that.

5. Give alcohol its own rule

If you drink, finish at least 3 hours before bed. It's the most common cause of "I slept 8 hours and feel awful."

6. Build a 30-minute wind-down

Same three or four low-stimulation things in the same order: shower, stretch, read, lights low. The routine itself becomes the signal — the point isn't the activities, it's the repetition.

7. Get out of bed if you're awake and frustrated

Lying there fuming teaches your brain that bed is a place for stress. After about 20 minutes, get up, sit somewhere dim and boring, and come back when you're sleepy.
