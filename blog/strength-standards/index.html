Strength Standards: How Strong Should You Be? (Charts & Levels)

"How strong should I be?" has a real answer — your bodyweight. These charts compare your squat, bench, deadlift and overhead press against beginner to elite levels, so you can find your level in 30 seconds and know exactly what to chase next.

Walk into any gym and you'll see someone squatting a bar loaded with plates, and someone else doing the same lift with just the bar. Neither of them knows whether they're "strong" — because strong compared to what?

Strength standards answer that question with data instead of vibes. They express your best lift as a multiple of your bodyweight, then place that number on a scale from beginner to elite. A 70 kg person squatting 105 kg and a 100 kg person squatting 150 kg are both at 1.5× bodyweight — the same level, despite very different plates. That's the whole idea, and it's why this comparison is fairer than "how much do you bench?"

This guide gives you the charts, the four lifts worth tracking, how to test your numbers without hurting yourself, and how to actually move up a level.

What Are Strength Standards?

A strength standard is a reference point for how much a person of a given bodyweight can typically lift for one repetition. Standards are built from huge databases of logged lifts and then grouped into levels, usually five of them: beginner, novice, intermediate, advanced and elite.

Because the number is a ratio, it scales with you. Two lifters at 1.25× bodyweight bench press are at the same relative level even if one weighs 60 kg and the other weighs 110 kg. This is the same logic as body fat percentage or TDEE: the raw number only means something once it's put in context.

The Only Formula You Need
Strength ratio = lift ÷ bodyweight
Use the same unit for both (kg or lb). Compare that ratio to the charts below. No conversions, no adjusting for age.

What standards are not

They are not a test you can fail. They're a population snapshot, and they're skewed by who logs lifts — mostly people who take training seriously. That means being "novice" is genuinely normal for a new lifter, and being "intermediate" already puts you ahead of most people who ever touch a barbell.

The Five Levels Explained

Each level corresponds to a rough percentile among people who train. The labels are consistent across the charts on this page.

Level Roughly stronger than Typical training time What it feels like
Beginner 5% of lifters 0–6 months Learning the movements, adding weight every session
Novice 20% 6–18 months Technique is solid, progress is weekly
Intermediate 50% 2–4 years Progress is monthly and needs a real plan
Advanced 80% 5+ years Small, hard-won gains; programming matters more than effort
Elite 95%+ 8–10+ years / competitive Competitive level, often weight-class specific

Training time assumes consistent work with reasonable programming. Two years of on-and-off training with no progression doesn't buy you the intermediate label — the clock only counts when you're actually showing up and adding load.

The Four Lifts That Matter

Standards focus on four barbell movements because between them they cover almost every major muscle group, they're easy to load progressively, and they're standardised enough to compare across gyms.

1. Back squat

The best single measure of lower-body strength, and the lift most people under-train. A squat counts when your thighs reach at least parallel to the floor — a quarter squat with 200 kg is not a 200 kg squat.

2. Bench press

The standard upper-body push. It's the lift most people care about and the one most affected by technique, arch and bar path, so a coach's five-minute fix can add kilos overnight.

3. Deadlift

The heaviest lift for almost everyone, and the one where bodyweight ratios run highest. It rewards patience and a strong setup more than aggression.

4. Overhead press

The honest lift. There's no bounce, no leg drive in a strict press, and progress is slow — which is exactly why hitting 0.8× bodyweight is a real milestone. If you're following a beginner strength programme, all four should appear in your week.

Notice the pattern: the gap between men's and women's numbers is biggest on the upper body and smallest on the deadlift. Use the table that matches you rather than discounting the other one by a fixed percentage — a discount flattens a real difference in how the two lifts behave.

⚠️ These are approximations, not rules. Standards vary by source, age group, limb length, technique standards and whether the lift was done in a competition. Use them to find your direction of travel, not to judge yourself.

How to Read Your Own Number

Three steps: find your best lift, divide by bodyweight, then find the band on the chart.

Example 1 — 80 kg man, 130 kg squat
Step 1: Best squat = 130 kg
Step 2: 130 ÷ 80 = 1.63× bodyweight
Step 3: Squat chart: 1.50× = intermediate, 2.25× = advanced
Result: comfortably intermediate, climbing toward advanced ✅
Example 2 — 65 kg woman, 100 kg deadlift
Step 1: Best deadlift = 100 kg
Step 2: 100 ÷ 65 = 1.54× bodyweight
Step 3: Women's deadlift chart: 1.50× = intermediate, 1.75× = advanced
Result: intermediate, about 14 kg away from advanced 💪

That last line is the useful part. A level isn't a badge, it's a distance. "14 kg away from advanced" tells you what to do next; "intermediate" on its own doesn't.

How to Test Your 1RM Safely

You don't need to load a bar to the point of failure to know your max. For most people, an estimate is accurate enough — and much safer.

  1. Use a rep-max estimate. Take a set you can do for 3–5 clean reps and put it into the 1RM formula guide, or skip the maths and use the one-rep max calculator.
  2. Warm up properly. If you do test a true single, work up in small jumps with a spotter or safety pins set at the right height.
  3. Keep it rare. A true 1RM test is a max-effort event. Once every 8–12 weeks is plenty, and not at all in your first few months of training.
  4. Judge the rep, not the number. A grinder with a rounded back isn't a valid max — it's a lift that will cost you two weeks of training.

How to Use Standards Without the Comparison Trap

Standards are a compass, not a scoreboard. The people who get the most from them use them in three specific ways:

  • Find your weakest lift. If your squat is intermediate and your press is beginner, your programme is unbalanced — that gap is your next six weeks of work.
  • Set a target with a date. "Bench 1× bodyweight in 12 weeks" is a plan. "Get stronger" isn't.
  • Compare to last quarter, not to Instagram. Your own trend is the only comparison that includes your sleep, your job, your joints and your starting point.

A few honest caveats: longer limbs make some lifts mechanically harder, lighter and heavier bodyweights change ratios, and age genuinely slows the top end. None of that changes what you should do this week — it just changes how much the label is worth.

How to Move Up a Level

The gap between levels is closed by boring, repeatable habits rather than heroic sessions:

1. Add load on purpose

Use a simple progression — add a small amount of weight when you hit the top of your rep range on every set. The mechanics of this are covered in progressive overload explained.

2. Eat enough, especially protein

Strength needs fuel and building material. Roughly 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight supports muscle repair; find your number with the protein calculator and read how much protein you actually need. If you're trying to lose fat and gain strength at the same time, body recomposition covers how to balance the two.

3. Sleep and recovery

Adaptation happens between sessions. Seven to nine hours of sleep and at least one full rest day per muscle group per week is not laziness, it's the other half of the programme.

4. Keep bodyweight stable-ish

Gaining weight can raise your absolute lifts while lowering your ratio. If the chart is your target, keep your weight steady and let the bar move instead.

Frequently Asked Questions
