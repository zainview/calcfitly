How to Calculate Your One Rep Max (1RM): Formulas, Chart & Guide

Every serious training program starts with one number. Here's how to find yours — without risking injury on a dangerous max-out attempt.

📅 Oct 18, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team
In this guide: What is a one rep max? Why you shouldn't max out The 1RM formulas (Epley & Brzycki) A worked example The reps-to-percentage chart Training percentages by goal How often to retest FAQs

Open any serious strength program and you'll see instructions like "squat 3 sets of 5 at 80%." But 80% of what?

The answer is your one rep max (1RM) — the single most important number in strength training. Let's find yours, safely.

What Is a One Rep Max?

Your one rep max is the maximum weight you can lift for exactly one full repetition of an exercise with proper form. It's the gold-standard measurement of maximal strength.

Why does it matter so much? Because it turns vague effort into precise programming:

  • Instead of "lift heavy," you get "lift 80% of 1RM"
  • Progress becomes measurable — your 1RM either went up or it didn't
  • Different goals (strength vs muscle vs endurance) each have proven percentage zones
  • You can compare strength fairly across time, and even against strength standards for your body weight

Why You Shouldn't Just Max Out

The obvious way to find your 1RM is to load the bar heavier and heavier until you can only do one rep. Here's why that's usually a bad idea:

  • Injury risk spikes. True max attempts push your form, joints, and nervous system to their absolute limit — often with imperfect technique under fatigue.
  • It requires experience. Beginners can't reliably express true maximal strength; their "1RM" attempt usually fails on technique, not strength.
  • It's extremely fatiguing. A real max-out session can disrupt your training for days.
  • It's unnecessary. Estimation formulas are accurate within 2-5% — close enough for any programming purpose.

The smarter way: lift a challenging but safe weight for multiple reps, then let math do the rest.

The 1RM Formulas (Epley & Brzycki)

Sports scientists have developed several formulas to predict 1RM from submaximal sets. The two most validated are:

Epley Formula (Most Popular)
1RM = Weight × (1 + Reps ÷ 30)
Best for rep ranges of 1-10. Slightly more generous estimates at higher reps.
Brzycki Formula
1RM = Weight × 36 ÷ (37 − Reps)
Often slightly more conservative. Very accurate for sets of 5-8 reps. Invalid at 37+ reps.

Both work well. For sets of 3-8 reps they typically agree within 1-3% — pick either, or average them for extra confidence. Our 1RM calculator runs multiple formulas automatically.

A Worked Example

🏋️ Estimating Bench Press 1RM
The test: You bench 80 kg for 6 clean reps (last rep hard but solid form)
Epley: 80 × (1 + 6/30) = 80 × 1.2 = 96 kg
Brzycki: 80 × 36/(37−6) = 80 × 36/31 = 92.9 kg
✅ Estimated 1RM: ~93-96 kg — program with ~94 kg as your working max

Pro tip: The fewer reps you use, the more accurate the estimate. A hard set of 3-5 predicts your max far better than a set of 12. Never estimate from sets beyond 10-12 reps — endurance varies too much between individuals.

Training Percentages by Goal

Now the payoff — using your 1RM to target specific adaptations:

Goal Intensity Rep Range
Maximal strength 85-95% 1RM 1-5 reps
Muscle growth (hypertrophy) 67-85% 1RM 6-12 reps
Muscular endurance Below 67% 1RM 12+ reps

Knowing your percentages is only half the system — the other half is increasing the load over time. Our guide on progressive overload shows you exactly when and how much to add, including the 2-for-2 rule.

Most general lifters should live mostly in the 70-85% range — heavy enough to build real strength, light enough to accumulate the volume that builds muscle. This is also the sweet spot if you're pursuing body recomposition, where muscle retention is everything.

How Often Should You Retest?

  • Beginners: Re-estimate every 6-8 weeks — your strength climbs fast early on
  • Intermediate: Every 8-12 weeks, or at the end of each training block
  • Advanced: Many simply re-estimate from regular heavy working sets instead of dedicated test days

And remember — strength isn't the only metric that matters. Pair your lifting numbers with body composition tracking for the complete picture, and fuel the work with enough protein (see how much protein you need).

The Bottom Line

Your 1RM is your strength compass — but you don't need a risky max-out to find it. Grab a weight you can lift for 3-8 clean reps, run the Epley formula (or let the calculator do it), and train off that number for the next two months. Retest, watch the number climb, and enjoy the most satisfying progress metric in fitness.

⚠️ A quick note: This article is for educational purposes only and is not medical advice. If you ever test heavy lifts, use a spotter or safety racks, warm up thoroughly, and stop if form breaks down. Consult a professional before starting maximal strength training.
