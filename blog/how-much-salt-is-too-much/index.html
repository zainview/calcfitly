How Much Salt Is Too Much? (Daily Limit + Hidden Sodium Chart)

Most people eat double the salt they should — and rarely from the salt shaker. Here are the actual limits, where the sodium is hiding, and how to cut back without your food turning bland.

Salt is the one nutrient where "a little" and "too much" are separated by a single teaspoon. It's also the one most people get wrong without noticing, because it arrives inside food rather than on top of it.

The short answer: keep salt under 5 to 6 grams a day — about 2,000 to 2,400 mg of sodium, or roughly one teaspoon. The World Health Organization says under 5 g, the UK sets 6 g, and the American Heart Association suggests aiming closer to 1,500 mg sodium (about 3.8 g salt) if your blood pressure is high.

Most adults eat 8 to 11 grams a day. So this isn't a "cut back slightly" situation — it's usually double.

One useful reality check before you start counting: a level teaspoon of salt weighs about 5.7 grams. That means the entire day's allowance is roughly one teaspoon — including everything already in your bread, pickle, papad, namkeen and restaurant food.

Salt vs Sodium: The Conversion

Labels in India and the US usually show sodium; European and UK labels often show salt. They are not the same number, and mixing them up makes people think they're within limits when they aren't.

The only conversion you need
Sodium (mg) = salt (g) × 400
Or: salt (g) = sodium (mg) ÷ 400. Salt is about 40% sodium by weight.
Reading a packet of instant noodles
Label says: sodium 1,450 mg per pack
Convert: 1,450 ÷ 400 ≈ 3.6 g salt
Against a 5 g daily limit: 72% of the day, in one snack
Add the tastemaker and you're essentially at the limit before dinner

None of these is "junk food" in the obvious sense. Bread and cheese are ordinary; pickle and papad are on every Indian table. Together, though, they can carry three or four grams of salt before a single grain comes out of the shaker.

Why Excess Salt Matters

Salt is not a toxin — your body needs sodium for nerve signals and fluid balance, roughly 500 mg a day to function. The problem is the dose, and what chronic excess does over years:

  • Blood pressure. Extra sodium makes your body hold water, which raises blood volume and pressure. About a third of people are especially salt-sensitive.
  • Stroke and heart disease. High blood pressure is the main pathway, and it's the leading modifiable risk factor for both.
  • Kidneys. Filtering a constant sodium load puts extra strain on them over decades, particularly with diabetes or existing kidney disease.
  • Stomach cancer. High-salt diets are associated with higher risk — one reason salt-preserved foods show up in the research.
  • Bone calcium. High sodium intake increases calcium loss in urine, which matters for bone health over time.
⚠️ Don't cut salt drastically if you have a medical condition, take diuretics, or train in extreme heat for hours. Sodium is also lost in sweat, and some people genuinely need more. Blood pressure medication and salt intake interact — talk to your doctor before big changes.

Salt, Water Weight and the Scale

Salt has no calories, so it cannot make you gain fat. It can, however, move the scale fast. Each gram of sodium holds roughly 200–300 ml of water, so a salty dinner can add 1–2 kg overnight. That's why people "lose 2 kg in 3 days" on a low-salt diet — it's water leaving, not fat.

The reverse is also useful to know: if you're dieting and the scale jumps after a restaurant meal, don't panic and cut calories further. Drink water, go back to your normal intake, and give it 2–3 days. For the bigger picture on what moves fat versus water, the sugar guide and hydration guide cover the neighbouring pieces of the same puzzle.

Potassium: The Other Half

Sodium and potassium work as a pair: sodium holds fluid, potassium helps your body release it. Most people eat too much sodium and too little potassium, which is a worse combination than either alone.

Aim for roughly 3,500 mg of potassium a day from food — bananas, potatoes, spinach, tomato, beans, curd, coconut water, oranges. You don't need supplements; a serving of dal, a potato and two servings of fruit covers most of it. This is the core idea behind DASH-style eating: not just less salt, but more potassium-rich whole food alongside it.

Can You Get Too Little Salt?

It's rarer than the opposite problem, but it happens — and it's usually self-inflicted by people who read "cut salt" and go to zero.

Your body needs roughly 500 mg of sodium a day just to run nerve signals and keep fluid balanced. Below that, and especially when you're also sweating heavily, you can run into:

  • Muscle cramps during or after training, particularly in heat
  • Dizziness or light-headedness on standing up
  • Unusual fatigue and headaches that don't lift with food or sleep
  • Hyponatraemia — dangerously low blood sodium, which happens when someone drinks large volumes of plain water during long endurance events without replacing salt

Who needs to pay attention: people training hard for more than an hour in the heat, heavy sweaters (visible salt lines on clothes), outdoor workers, and anyone on a very low-salt diet who also drinks a lot of water. For them, a pinch of salt in the bottle or an electrolyte drink during long sessions is sensible, not a cheat.

For everyone else sitting at a desk in air conditioning, the risk of too little salt is close to zero on a normal diet. If you're in doubt, the safe middle is simple: cook with a normal amount of salt, keep packaged food occasional, and don't chase a zero-sodium target that no health body recommends.

How to Read a Label in 5 Seconds

Look at the per-100-gram column:

Per 100 g Verdict
Salt over 1.5 g (sodium over 600 mg)High — occasional, not daily
Salt 0.3–1.5 g (sodium 120–600 mg)Medium — check your serving size
Salt 0.3 g or less (sodium 120 mg or less)Low — good everyday choice

Then check the serving count. A 70 g pack of chips listing "2 servings" means the numbers on the front are half of what you'll actually eat. The same trick shows up on namkeen, biscuits and instant noodles.

7 Ways to Cut Salt Without Bland Food

Your taste for salt is learned, and it resets. Most people stop missing the excess within two to four weeks if they reduce gradually rather than overnight.

  1. Reduce cooking salt by a quarter, not by half. Do it for two weeks, then reduce again. Gradual cuts go unnoticed; sudden ones make food taste like cardboard.
  2. Add salt at the end, not throughout. Salt on the surface hits your tongue first, so you need less of it for the same taste.
  3. Use acid to fill the gap. Lemon on dal, vinegar in salads, tamarind in curries — sourness reads as "flavour" even when salt is low.
  4. Lean on aromatics and roasted spices. Garlic, ginger, onion, jeera, coriander, black pepper, curry leaves — all salt-free and all stronger when the food is less salty.
  5. Rinse what you can. Canned beans and chickpeas lose a noticeable amount of sodium when rinsed for 30 seconds.
  6. Keep papad, pickle and namkeen as occasional items. Not banned — just not at every meal. One tablespoon of pickle is nearly a gram of salt.
  7. Taste before adding. The most common salt source is habit: adding it before trying the food. Taste first, then decide.
