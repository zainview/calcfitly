How Much Water Should You Drink a Day? (Chart by Weight)

"Eight glasses" is a rough guess, not a rule. Your real number depends on bodyweight, training and heat — here's the chart, the formula behind it, and how to tell whether you're actually hydrated.

Almost everyone has heard "drink 8 glasses a day." It's memorable, it's easy, and for a lot of people it's roughly right — but a 50 kg woman who works at a desk and a 95 kg man who trains five times a week have very different needs, and both are told the same number.

The honest answer: most adults need about 2 to 3 litres of fluid a day, which works out to roughly 30–35 ml per kilogram of bodyweight. That total includes water, tea, coffee, milk and the water in food. Add more on training days and in hot weather.

Here's how to calculate your number, what actually moves it, and the signs that you're running dry long before you feel thirsty.

How Much Water Do You Actually Need?

The European Food Safety Authority puts total water intake at about 2 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres for men, and large surveys in the US land in a similar place. But those are population averages for average-sized, moderately active people.

A weight-based estimate personalises it in one step:

Daily fluid target
Bodyweight (kg) × 30–35 ml
80 kg × 32 ml ≈ 2.6 litres. Add 0.5–1 L per hour of hard training, and more in heat.
Example: 72 kg, trains 4× a week
Baseline: 72 × 32 ml ≈ 2.3 litres
Training day, 1 hour lifting: +0.5 litre
Hot day or lots of walking: +0.3 litre
Target: ~2.8–3.1 litres on training days, 2.3 on rest days

Notice how wide the top end gets: a 100 kg person may need almost double what a 50 kg person needs. That's exactly why one universal number fails.

Why "8 Glasses" Is Only a Starting Point

Eight glasses is roughly 2 litres — a decent default for an average adult. It becomes wrong in two directions:

  • Too much for small, sedentary people, who end up running to the bathroom all day and feeling bloated.
  • Not enough for bigger, active people, who spend their afternoons mildly dehydrated without realising it.

There's also the food side. Roughly 20–30 percent of your daily water comes from what you eat — fruit, vegetables, yoghurt, soup, rice, dal. Someone eating a lot of produce needs less from the bottle than someone living on dry snacks.

What Adds to Your Number

Training

Add 0.5 to 1 litre per hour of hard exercise. You lose fluid through sweat and breathing, and the loss is bigger than most people assume — a sweaty hour of lifting or a run can cost 0.5–1.5 litres. If you want to be precise, weigh yourself before and after a session: every kilogram lost is about a litre, and you should replace 125–150 percent of it in the next few hours.

Heat and humidity

Sweat rate can double or triple in hot weather. In summer or a poorly ventilated gym, treat your baseline as the floor, not the target.

Protein and fibre

A high-protein diet — like the ranges in our protein guide — needs more water, because the kidneys use fluid to clear nitrogen waste. The same applies to a high-fibre diet. Neither is a problem; both simply raise your fluid needs.

Alcohol and very salty food

Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, the hormone that tells your kidneys to hold water, so you urinate more than you drank. Salty restaurant food pulls water into your tissues and leaves you thirsty. Both are reasons to drink extra, not to "balance" with less.

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and illness

Pregnancy adds about 0.3 litre a day; breastfeeding adds roughly 0.7 litre. Fever, vomiting and diarrhoea raise needs sharply — that's medical territory, so follow your doctor's advice rather than a chart.

How to Tell If You're Hydrated

Thirst is a late signal — by the time you feel it, you're already down 1–2 percent of bodyweight in fluid. Urine colour is the fastest everyday check:

Urine colour What it means What to do
Pale strawWell hydratedNothing — keep going
Light yellowFine, slightly lowOne glass now
Bright or dark yellowMildly dehydrated2–3 glasses over the next hour
Amber / apple juiceDehydratedDrink steadily, not all at once
Brown or tea-colouredBadly dehydrated or medicalHydrate and see a doctor if it persists

Other checks that show up before thirst: a dull headache by mid-afternoon, dry mouth, dark and strong-smelling urine, constipation, and feeling unusually tired during a workout. Our guide to the early signs of dehydration goes deeper if you keep hitting these.

Can You Drink Too Much Water?

Yes, though it's rare outside endurance events. Your kidneys clear roughly 0.8–1 litre per hour; drink far beyond that and blood sodium gets diluted, which can cause hyponatraemia — nausea, confusion, headaches and in severe cases swelling of the brain.

This almost never happens from sipping through the day. It happens when people drink several litres in a short window, usually during a marathon, a hot workout or a "water challenge." Spread your intake out, and don't force litres past thirst just to hit a number.

⚠️ People with heart, kidney or liver conditions should not use a weight-based formula. Fluid limits in those cases are individual and set by a doctor.

Timing: Hitting Your Target Without Chugging

The most common reason people miss their target isn't thirst — it's forgetfulness. A simple schedule beats willpower:

Time Amount Why
On waking300–500 mlYou lose fluid overnight through breathing
With each meal250–350 mlAnchors three servings to habits you already have
Before training400–600 ml, 1–2 h priorGives your body time to absorb it
During training150–250 ml every 15–20 minReplaces sweat without bloating
After training500 ml + what you sweatedRecovery, especially before the next session
EveningSmall sips onlyProtects sleep — see our sleep guide

Two practical tricks: keep a 750 ml bottle on your desk and refill it three times, or mark a 1 litre bottle with times (9am, 12pm, 3pm, 6pm). Both turn an abstract number into a visible checklist.

Do You Need Electrolytes?

For most everyday situations, no. Food covers it — salt in your meals, potassium from fruit and vegetables, and a normal diet supply everything you lose in light sweat.

Electrolytes start to matter when you sweat heavily for a long time:

  • More than 60–90 minutes of hard training, especially in heat
  • Very high sweat rates — visible salt lines on your clothes, or sweat that stings your eyes
  • Back-to-back sessions, like two matches in a day or a long ride followed by a gym session

In those cases, a pinch of salt in your bottle or an electrolyte tablet helps you hold onto the water you drink instead of losing it straight away. Sweat contains roughly 300–700 mg of sodium per litre, so plain water alone can leave you feeling sloshy and still thirsty.

The opposite mistake is common too: drinking large volumes of plain water during a hot, long session without any sodium. That's the setup for the hyponatraemia we covered above — headaches, nausea and confusion. If you're sweating for hours, replace salt as well as water.

Sports drinks are fine for long or hot sessions, but for a 45-minute gym workout they mostly add sugar you don't need. Water plus a normal meal afterwards does the job.

Water and Fat Loss: What It Actually Does

You'll see claims that cold water "boosts metabolism" or that drinking 4 litres a day "melts fat." The real effect is smaller, but it isn't zero:

  • It replaces liquid calories. Swapping two soft drinks or a sugary coffee for water can cut 200–400 calories a day without any feeling of restriction. This is the biggest and most reliable effect.
  • It can trim intake slightly before meals. Studies where people drank about 500 ml before eating show a modest reduction in how much they ate. Not dramatic, but free.
  • It separates thirst from hunger. Mild dehydration often shows up as a snack craving, especially mid-afternoon. A glass of water and ten minutes usually answers the question.
  • It keeps training quality up. Even 2 percent dehydration measurably reduces strength endurance and makes a session feel harder, which affects how consistently you show up.

What water does not do is change the maths of a calorie deficit. If your weight has stalled, check the bigger levers first — your TDEE, your portion sizes and how honestly you're tracking — then use hydration as support. And ignore the scale jumps after a salty meal or a hard session: that's water shifting, not fat.
