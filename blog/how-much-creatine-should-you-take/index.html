How Much Creatine Should You Take a Day?

Creatine is the most studied supplement in sport, and also the most over-complicated. The dose that works fits in a teaspoon. Here is exactly how much to take, whether loading is worth it, and what the safety evidence actually shows.

📅 Nov 22, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team

Creatine has been studied in hundreds of trials since the 1990s, and the findings have barely moved: it increases strength and lean mass modestly, it is safe in healthy people, and almost everyone takes far more of it than they need to — or worries about it far more than the evidence justifies.

The short answer: 3 to 5 grams of creatine monohydrate every day. The body-weight version is about 0.03 g per kilogram, so a 70 kg adult needs roughly 2 grams — but a 5 gram scoop covers every body size, and it is the dose used in most research. One scoop, any time of day, forever. That is the whole protocol.

The confusion usually comes from three places: loading, timing, and the fear of kidneys. All three are worth ten minutes of understanding, which is what this guide is for.

How Much Creatine Per Day?

Your muscles hold a limited pool of creatine, and the supplement only helps once that pool is full. Maintenance dosing replaces what your body breaks down each day — roughly 1 to 2 grams for a typical adult — plus a small buffer. That is why 3 to 5 grams a day is enough no matter how hard you train.

Muscle creatine stores max out at about 150 to 160 mmol/kg of dry muscle. Once you are there, extra creatine is simply excreted in urine. Taking 10 grams instead of 5 does not build more muscle — it just costs twice as much.

Dose by Body Weight

Larger bodies hold more muscle, so the official recommendation is dosed per kilogram: 0.03 g/kg per day to maintain, and 0.3 g/kg per day while loading. The table shows both, plus the practical scoop version.

50 kg1.5 g3 g15 g (4 × 3.75 g)
60 kg1.8 g3 g18 g (4 × 4.5 g)
70 kg2.1 g3–4 g21 g (4 × 5 g)

100 kg3.0 g5 g30 g (4 × 7.5 g)

Notice how small the gap is between a 50 kg and a 100 kg person. This is not a supplement where more is better — it is one where enough is enough. If you are vegetarian or vegan, your baseline muscle creatine is lower, so you may notice a bigger response and should be consistent with the daily dose rather than skipping days.

Creatine dose
maintenance = body weight (kg) × 0.03
Loading = body weight × 0.3, split into 4 doses a day for 5–7 days. A 5 g scoop of monohydrate delivers about 4.4 g of actual creatine.

Loading vs No Loading

Loading is the fast route to full muscles. Instead of 3 to 5 grams, you take about 20 grams a day — four doses of 5 grams, spread across the day — for 5 to 7 days. Then you drop to the normal dose.

Without loading, the same 3 to 5 grams a day fills your muscles in about 3 to 4 weeks. Both routes end at the same place. Loading just gets you there sooner, which only matters if you have a competition or a test in the next two weeks.

Approach Daily dose Time to full muscles Trade-off
Loading20 g, split 4×5–7 daysMore bloating and loose stools
No loading3–5 g once3–4 weeksSlower start, easier on the stomach

Two myths to close out here. First, you do not need to cycle creatine — no 8-weeks-on, 4-weeks-off pattern. Your muscles do not "get used to it" and stop responding. Second, loading is not a requirement for results, and for most people starting out, the slow route is the more comfortable one.

⚠️ Split the load. A single 20 gram dose mostly ends up in your gut, which is why it causes cramps and diarrhoea. Four 5 gram doses with meals avoids most of that.

Which Creatine to Buy

Creatine monohydrate. That is the answer, and it has not changed in twenty years of research.

  • Monohydrate — the cheapest, the most studied, and the form used in almost every trial that shows a benefit. A 300 g tub lasts two months at 5 g a day.
  • Creatine HCl — marketed as more soluble and gentler. No study shows it works better than monohydrate, and it costs three to five times more.
  • Ethyl ester, buffered, liquid — the ethyl ester converts to creatinine in the gut and delivers less creatine, not more.
  • Blends with flavour and sugar — fine if you like the taste, but you are paying for the flavour, not the creatine.

Look for "creatine monohydrate" and, if possible, a Creapure or third-party tested label. Micronised monohydrate dissolves a little better and is easier on the stomach for some people, which is the only meaningful upgrade worth paying for.

When to Take It

Anytime. Consistency beats timing, and this is one of the few supplement questions where the research is genuinely boring: total daily intake is what fills your muscles, not the clock.

A few small points worth knowing:

  • After training, with a meal — a handful of trials found a slight advantage over taking it before training, likely because insulin helps pull creatine into muscle. The effect is small; do not restructure your day for it.
  • With carbs and protein — a meal containing both raises insulin, and that improves retention slightly compared to taking it on an empty stomach with water.
  • Rest days count — muscle saturation is maintained by daily intake, not by training days. Take it every day, including the days you skip the gym.
  • Caffeine is fine — the old claim that caffeine cancels creatine comes from a single small study and has not held up. Coffee and creatine coexist.

The Weight Gain Question

The scale will probably move up by 1 to 2 kg in the first two weeks. That is water, and it is inside the muscle, not under the skin — creatine pulls water into muscle cells, which is part of why the muscle looks fuller.

This is not fat gain, and it is not "bloating" in the sense of a puffy face. If you are dieting and tracking weight, expect the jump and do not panic-adjust your calories because of it. After the initial shift, weight change follows your diet and training as usual.

Over months, the research shows a modest gain in lean mass on top of training — typically around 1 to 2 kg more than training alone in a well-fed beginner. That is a real but small effect, and it is why creatine is not a shortcut.

Safety and Side Effects

In healthy people, creatine monohydrate has been studied for up to five years with no evidence of harm to kidneys, liver or blood pressure. The International Society of Sports Nutrition considers it safe at 3 to 5 grams a day for long-term use.

The realistic side effects:

  • Stomach upset — the most common one, usually from a large single dose. Split it or take it with food.
  • Raised creatinine on a blood test — creatine breaks down into creatinine, so the number looks higher. It does not mean your kidneys are failing, but tell your doctor before a kidney panel so nobody misreads it.
  • Thirst — mild for some people. Drink to your normal thirst; there is no need to force litres of water.
  • Hair loss — no evidence. The claim traces back to one 2009 rugby study measuring DHT, and it has never been replicated.
⚠️ If you have kidney disease, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take medication that affects kidney function, talk to your doctor before starting. The safety data is for healthy adults.

Who Benefits Most

Creatine is not magic and roughly 20 to 30 percent of people are low responders — their baseline muscle creatine is already high, so there is less room to improve. The groups who respond most reliably:

  • Strength and power athletes — sprinting, lifting, jumping, repeated efforts. The effect is on short bursts, not endurance.
  • Vegetarians and vegans — dietary creatine comes from meat and fish, so baseline stores are lower and the response tends to be larger.
  • Older adults — combined with resistance training, 3 to 5 grams a day supports muscle mass and function, which matters for falls and independence.
  • Anyone in a calorie deficit — it helps preserve lean mass while you lose fat, which is the closest thing to a "fat loss" benefit it has.
  • Women — same dose, same response. No cycle-based dosing is needed.

What it will not do: burn fat on its own, replace protein, or fix a bad programme. If you are not progressively overloading, read how progressive overload works before spending money on supplements.

Creatine in Food

Creatine is found almost entirely in meat and fish, which is why vegetarians have lower stores. The amounts are real but small — herring is the outlier.

You would need over a kilogram of beef a day to match one 5 gram scoop, and cooking reduces the creatine content further as some of it converts to creatinine. Food is not a practical substitute for the supplement, though it does explain why a mixed diet keeps you partly topped up.

How to Take It, Practically

Five habits make this effortless:

  1. Keep the scoop in the tub and take 5 g with your first meal. Attach it to something you already do every day.
  2. Mix in water, milk or a shake — 200 ml is plenty. It does not dissolve completely; that grit is normal.
  3. Do not pre-mix for later. Creatine in liquid slowly converts to creatinine, which is useless. Mix and drink.
  4. Keep the tub dry. A wet scoop turns the powder into a brick. Use a dry spoon.
  5. Buy 250–300 g at a time. At 5 g a day that is six to eight weeks, and it keeps the cost around ₹1–2 per day for most Indian brands.
