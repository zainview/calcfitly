How Many Carbs Should You Eat a Day? (By Goal & Activity)

Carbs aren't the enemy and "low-carb" isn't a personality. Here's the number that actually fits your training, your calorie target and your goal — in grams, not vague percentages.

📅 Nov 9, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team

Carb advice swings between two extremes: "carbs are fuel, eat more" and "carbs are sugar, cut them." Both are half-right, which is why the question keeps coming back.

The short answer: most adults do well on 45–65 percent of calories from carbohydrates — roughly 3 to 7 grams per kilogram of bodyweight per day. If you train hard, aim toward the top of that range. If you sit most of the day and train twice a week, aim lower. On a 2,000 calorie diet, 45–65 percent works out to 225–325 grams.

Here's how to turn that into a number you can actually eat, why carbs don't make you fat on their own, and where fibre and timing fit in.

How Many Carbs Do You Actually Need?

The official minimum for brain function is about 130 grams a day — that's a floor, not a target. The Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range puts carbs at 45–65 percent of calories, which matches what most traditional diets (rice, roti, dal, potatoes, fruit) naturally deliver.

But percentages hide the thing that matters: how much you train. Muscle glycogen is the fuel for hard effort, and its capacity scales with body size and training load. That's why serious athletes eat 6–10 g/kg while a desk worker is fine on 3–4 g/kg.

Quick estimate
Carbs (g) = bodyweight (kg) × grams per kg
Sedentary 3–5 · moderate training 5–7 · hard training 6–10 · athlete 8–12. Then check it against your calorie target.
Example: 70 kg, trains 4× a week, wants fat loss
Calorie target: 2,000 kcal
Protein first: 140 g (560 kcal)
Fat: 60 g (540 kcal)
Remaining calories: 900 kcal → 225 g carbs
70 kg × 3.2 g/kg ≈ 225 g — the two methods agree

Two honest notes. First, the top rows look huge because they are — a 70 kg endurance athlete genuinely eats 600+ grams of carbs on long training days. Second, if you're dieting, you don't need the athlete range even if you train a lot; the deficit takes priority and carbs drop first.

For fat loss specifically, 40–50 percent is a comfortable landing zone: enough carbs to train well, low enough to keep the deficit. Below about 100 g a day you're in low-carb territory — it can work, but it's a choice, not a requirement.

Do Carbs Make You Fat?

No. Body fat changes when calories in and calories out drift apart over weeks, not because of one macronutrient. Controlled studies comparing low-carb and high-carb diets at the same calories and protein find similar fat loss.

What carbs do have going against them:

  • They're easy to overeat. Chips, biscuits, sweet drinks and white bread are calorie-dense and not very filling. A 500 kcal bag of snacks disappears in five minutes; 500 kcal of dal and vegetables is a full meal.
  • They hold water. Every gram of stored glycogen comes with roughly 3 grams of water. Start eating more carbs and the scale jumps 1–2 kg in days — that's water, not fat.
  • Refined carbs spike appetite. Fast-digesting, low-fibre carbs leave you hungry sooner, which makes a deficit harder to hold.

So the practical rule isn't "eat fewer carbs" — it's "eat fewer calories, and get most of your carbs from sources that come with fibre."

Fibre: The Part People Skip

Fibre is a carbohydrate, and it's the one most people are short on. Target 25–35 grams a day. It slows digestion, feeds gut bacteria, improves cholesterol and keeps you full — which is why high-fibre diets make calorie deficits feel easier.

Where it comes from, per realistic serving:

  • Dal (1 katori, cooked): ~5–7 g
  • Rajma / chickpeas (1 katori): ~6–8 g
  • Oats (40 g dry): ~4 g
  • Whole wheat roti (1 medium): ~3 g
  • Fruit (apple, guava, pear): ~3–5 g each
  • Vegetables (1 cup cooked): ~3–5 g

If you're moving from a low-fibre diet, increase gradually and drink more water — sudden jumps cause bloating. Our fibre guide covers the full list and the timing tricks.

Timing Around Training

Total daily carbs matter more than timing, but if you train hard, placement helps:

  • 2–3 hours before: a normal meal with 40–80 g of carbs and some protein. Rice, roti, oats, fruit — nothing exotic.
  • 30–60 minutes before: 20–40 g of easy carbs if you're training fasted or early morning.
  • After: 0.5–1 g/kg of carbs plus 20–40 g protein within a couple of hours. For an 80 kg person, that's 40–80 g of carbs.
  • Rest days: no need to force carbs around a workout that isn't happening.

If you train twice in a day, the post-session window matters much more — glycogen has to be refilled before the next session. For a single daily workout, hitting your daily total is enough.

You don't have to be strict. An 80/20 split — whole foods most of the time, flexible the rest — is easier to keep for a year than a perfect plan you abandon in three weeks. White rice isn't "bad"; it just carries less fibre, so pair it with dal and vegetables.

💡 Carbs and protein together. Pairing carb sources with 20–30 g of protein per meal slows digestion, keeps you fuller and protects muscle in a deficit. Our protein guide has the per-meal numbers.

How to Set Your Carbs in 5 Steps

Percentages are fine on paper; grams on a plate are what matter. Work in this order and the maths resolves itself:

  1. Fix calories first. Maintenance from the TDEE minus 300–500 for fat loss, or plus 200–300 for a lean gain. Nothing else works until this number is set.
  2. Set protein. 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight. It protects muscle in a deficit and keeps you full — this is the one macro worth getting exactly right.
  3. Set fat. About 0.6–1 g per kg, or roughly 20–30 percent of calories. Go below that for weeks and hormones and mood usually notice.
  4. Carbs take the remainder. Subtract protein and fat calories from your total, divide what's left by 4, and you have your grams. This is the only macro that floats.
  5. Check fibre and energy. 25–35 g of fibre a day, and workouts that feel normal. If training is flat for two weeks, move 30–50 g from fat to carbs before changing anything else.

One example of the logic: 2,200 kcal, 75 kg, training four times a week. Protein 150 g (600 kcal), fat 65 g (585 kcal), leaving 1,015 kcal — about 254 g of carbs, or 3.4 g/kg. That sits neatly in the moderate-training range, and it is roughly what a normal Indian diet of roti, rice, dal and fruit already delivers.

Track for two weeks, then stop tracking and eat the same meals by habit. The number is a starting point, not a life sentence — if your weight and training are moving the right way, the exact gram count doesn't need to be perfect.
