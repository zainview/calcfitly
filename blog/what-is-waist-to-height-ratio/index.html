Waist-to-Height Ratio: The 0.5 Rule (Chart & How to Measure)

One tape measure, one division, no scale needed. Here's the number that tells you whether your belly fat is actually putting your health at risk — and exactly how to work it out at home.

📅 Nov 6, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team
In this guide: What is waist-to-height ratio? How to measure your waist correctly The waist-to-height ratio chart Why it beats BMI for belly fat Worked examples What to do if your ratio is 0.5 or higher What it can't tell you FAQs

You can be a perfectly normal weight and still carry too much fat around your middle. That's the blind spot in every bathroom scale, and it's the reason doctors increasingly reach for a tape measure instead of a weight chart.

The waist-to-height ratio is the simplest version of that idea. It takes about thirty seconds, needs no equipment beyond a tape, and gives you one number you can track for the rest of your life. This guide covers the formula, the famous 0.5 rule, how to measure without fooling yourself, and what to actually do if your number comes back high.

What Is Waist-to-Height Ratio?

Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) compares the size of your waist to your height. Instead of asking "how much do I weigh?", it asks a more useful question: is my waist small enough for the body I'm standing in?

The Formula
WHtR = waist ÷ height
Use the same unit for both — centimetres or inches. No squaring, no conversions, no BMI-style charts.

Because it's a ratio, the same cut-off works for men and women of most heights and ethnic backgrounds. That's rare in health screening, and it's why public health bodies like it.

The 0.5 Rule, In One Line

Keep your waist to less than half your height. If your waist measures half your height or more, you're carrying more fat around your organs than is ideal.

For a 170 cm (5'7") person, half of 170 is 85 cm — so the target is a waist under 85 cm (about 33 inches). For someone 180 cm (5'11"), it's under 90 cm (about 35.5 inches). That's it. No table required, though there is one below if you'd rather not do the division.

How to Measure Your Waist Correctly

Most people measure in the wrong place, or over a jumper, or while holding their stomach in. Any of those can shift the number by several centimetres — enough to move you a whole category.

  1. Stand tall and relaxed. Feet together, arms down, breathe normally. Don't suck in.
  2. Find the midpoint. Locate the bottom of your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, then measure halfway between them. For most people this is roughly level with the navel, but follow your own body, not the navel.
  3. Wrap the tape level. It should sit flat and horizontal all the way around — not dipping at the back.
  4. Snug, not tight. The tape should touch your skin without pressing into it. You should still be able to breathe out fully.
  5. Measure at the end of a normal exhale, then repeat two more times and average the readings.

Common mistakes to avoid: measuring over clothing, measuring at the narrowest point (that's your natural waist, not your waist circumference), pulling the tape tight to "get a better number", and measuring right after a big meal or a bloated day.

📏 Measure at the same time of day each time — morning, before food, is the most consistent. Your waist can vary 1–2 cm in a day from food, water and bloating, and that's normal, not fat gain.

The Waist-to-Height Ratio Chart

Health guidance in the UK classifies central adiposity (belly fat) using three bands. The wording matters less than the message: under 0.5 is where you want to be.

Waist-to-height ratio Category What it means
Below 0.40 Below healthy range Waist unusually small for your height. Worth checking whether you're underweight using BMI.
0.40 – 0.49 Healthy No increased health risk from belly fat. The goal is to stay here.
0.50 – 0.59 Increased risk Enough central fat to raise risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease. This is the range to act on.
0.60 and above High risk Further increased risk. Worth a conversation with a healthcare professional.

One caution: the lower bound matters too. A ratio below 0.40 isn't a target to chase — it usually just means you're quite slim, and going lower doesn't buy extra health.

Why It Beats BMI for Belly Fat

BMI compares your total weight to your height. It has no idea whether that weight is muscle, hip fat or fat wrapped around your liver and intestines — and the location is what drives most of the risk.

Visceral fat, the fat stored deep around your organs, is metabolically active in the worst way. It's associated with insulin resistance, higher blood pressure, raised triglycerides and inflammation. A tape measure catches it; a scale doesn't.

Measure What it tells you Weakness
BMI Weight relative to height Can't see where fat sits; flags muscular people as overweight
Waist circumference Absolute belly size Needs different cut-offs for men, women and ethnic groups
Waist-to-height ratio Belly size scaled to your frame Still can't separate visceral from subcutaneous fat

The evidence is fairly consistent. Analyses pooling dozens of studies have found waist-to-height ratio discriminates cardiometabolic risk at least as well as BMI and waist circumference, and better in many comparisons. In survey data from England, around 31% of adults with a "healthy" BMI still had a waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 or above — meaning a normal BMI quietly hid a raised risk. A large UK Biobank analysis also found that a ratio of 0.5 or higher was linked to higher risk of ischaemic heart disease in both sexes.

That's why current UK guidance says to use waist-to-height ratio alongside BMI for anyone with a BMI under 35, across both sexes and all ethnicities — including people with a lot of muscle. For more on BMI's limits, see Is BMI Accurate? and BMI vs Body Fat.

Worked Examples

📐 Example 1: Priya, 5'6"
Step 1: Height = 66 inches (168 cm)
Step 2: Waist = 34 inches (86 cm)
Step 3: 34 ÷ 66 = 0.515
Step 4: Half her height = 33 inches → target waist under 33 in
⚠️ WHtR = 0.52 — increased risk. She needs to lose about 1 inch from her waist to reach 0.49.
📐 Example 2: Arjun, 5'11"
Step 1: Height = 71 inches (180 cm)
Step 2: Waist = 38 inches (97 cm)
Step 3: 38 ÷ 71 = 0.535
Step 4: Half his height = 35.5 inches → target waist under 35.5 in
⚠️ WHtR = 0.54 — increased risk. Around 2.5 inches off the waist puts him in the healthy band.

Notice how small the gap is. Neither person needs a dramatic transformation — a couple of inches around the middle is the difference between a raised-risk reading and a healthy one. For most people that's 3–5 kg of fat loss, not 20.

What to Do If Your Ratio Is 0.5 or Higher

Belly fat responds to the same three levers as any other fat, just with a bias toward the first two:

1. Eat in a small calorie deficit

You can't target belly fat with exercise — spot reduction doesn't work. What does work is a consistent deficit of roughly 300–500 calories a day. Not sure where to start? Read What Is a Calorie Deficit? and then find your maintenance number with the TDEE calculator.

2. Lift weights and keep protein high

Strength training protects muscle while you lose fat, which keeps your metabolism from sliding and improves how your waist looks at the same weight. Aim for two to three sessions a week and roughly 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight. Start here: Strength Training for Beginners.

3. Move more across the whole day

Daily steps and general movement (NEAT) often matter more than formal cardio for fat loss, because it's easier to sustain. A 7,000–9,000 step baseline is a realistic target. See What Is NEAT? and Walking for Weight Loss.

Then track it properly: measure your waist every four weeks under the same conditions, and expect 1–2 cm per month. Waist change often shows up before the scale moves, which is exactly why it's worth measuring.

What It Can't Tell You

  • Visceral vs subcutaneous fat. A tape measure can't separate the fat around your organs from the fat just under your skin. Only imaging can.
  • Muscle at the waist. Heavy lifters can have a thicker midsection from muscle and core work. Compare against your own trend, not just the chart.
  • Pregnancy and children. Both need different charts — this article is for non-pregnant adults only.
  • Single readings. Bloating, posture and even time of day move the number. Trends over 3–4 measurements matter more than one.
  • A diagnosis. It's a screening signal, not a medical verdict. Blood pressure, blood sugar and family history all count too.

If your ratio is 0.6 or above, or your waist has grown noticeably in a short space of time, speak to a doctor — especially if you also have high blood pressure, high blood sugar or a family history of heart disease.
