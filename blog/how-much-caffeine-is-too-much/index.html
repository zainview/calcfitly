How Much Caffeine Is Too Much? (Daily Limit + Cup Chart)

400 mg is the line for most adults — about four cups of coffee. But the number that matters more is what's still in your system at bedtime. Here are the limits, the cup-by-cup numbers, and how to cut back without a headache.

Caffeine is the one drug most people take daily without calling it one. It works, it's cheap, and the line between "helpful" and "why is my heart doing that" is more specific than most people realise.

The short answer: up to 400 mg a day is considered safe for healthy adults — roughly four 240 ml cups of brewed coffee. Both the FDA and the European Food Safety Authority land on that number. Pregnancy halves it to 200 mg, and adolescents should stay under 100 mg.

But 400 mg is a ceiling, not a target. And the timing question — when your last cup is — often matters more than the total.

Two clarifications people usually need. First, the single-dose limit is separate: 400 mg spread across a day feels very different from 400 mg in an hour. Second, some people are slow metabolisers — a genetic variation means caffeine hangs around roughly twice as long, and for them even 200 mg can wreck sleep and cause anxiety.

Half-Life: Why Your 4pm Coffee Matters

Caffeine has a half-life of about five hours in most people. That means half of it is gone five hours later, half of what remains is gone in another five — and so on.

The maths of a 4pm coffee (95 mg)
9pm → ~48 mg · 2am → ~24 mg · 7am → ~12 mg
Even small amounts at bedtime can reduce deep sleep, which is the part that matters for recovery.

So the question isn't only "how much today" but "how much is still on board tonight". A practical rule: keep your last caffeine 8–10 hours before bed. If you sleep at 11pm, that means a 1–3pm cutoff. Most people find that unpleasant for a week and then don't miss it.

Tolerance and Withdrawal

Regular caffeine use changes your brain. Adenosine receptors increase, which means the same dose does less — that's tolerance. It's why the third cup stops feeling like the first one did.

Withdrawal is real and well documented: headache, fatigue, irritability, poor concentration and sometimes flu-like feelings, starting 12–24 hours after your last dose and peaking around 20–48 hours. It usually resolves in two to nine days. The headache is mostly from dilated blood vessels in the brain, which is why a small dose relieves it within 30 minutes — and why quitting cold turkey is unnecessarily painful.

Caffeine and Sleep

This is where caffeine does its most expensive damage, because it's invisible. Sleep can look fine — you fall asleep, you stay asleep — while deep sleep quietly shrinks. Over weeks that shows up as lower training quality, more appetite and a flatter mood.

Three things help: move the cutoff earlier, reduce the afternoon dose rather than the morning one, and keep your wake time fixed so your body clock stays anchored. If you're already sleeping badly, the sleep hours guide and the sleep calculator will tell you what bedtime actually fits your alarm.

Caffeine and Training

Caffeine genuinely works as a performance aid: roughly 3–6 mg per kg of bodyweight, taken 30–60 minutes before training, improves power output, endurance and perceived effort. For a 70 kg person that's 210–420 mg — right at the daily ceiling, which is why it's usually reserved for hard sessions or competition rather than every workout.

The catch is tolerance. Daily pre-workout coffee blunts the effect within a couple of weeks. Using caffeine strategically — big dose on hard days, low or none on easy days — keeps it useful.

Does Caffeine Help Fat Loss?

Marginally, and not enough to plan around. Caffeine raises metabolic rate by a small amount for a few hours and can slightly reduce appetite. Studies on green tea extract show effects so small they're within measurement noise for most people.

What actually moves fat loss is the calorie deficit, and caffeine's real contribution there is indirect: it makes training feel easier, and it replaces sugary drinks if you drink it black. A latte with three sugars is a dessert, not a fat burner.

Signs You're Over Your Limit

The 400 mg figure is a population ceiling. Your personal line can be lower, and the signs usually show up before the total gets close to it:

Signal What it usually means
Jitters, shaky handsToo much at once — spread the same total across the day
Racing or pounding heartYou're past your personal threshold; a slow metaboliser issue
Anxiety, irritability, restlessnessCaffeine amplifying stress response — cut the afternoon dose first
Headache by mid-morningWithdrawal, not caffeine effect — you're on a dependency cycle
Waking at 3am, light sleepEvening caffeine still on board — move the cutoff earlier
Needing it to feel normalTolerance — the dose no longer does much except prevent withdrawal

If two or more of these sound familiar, the fix isn't necessarily quitting. It's usually: same total, smaller doses, earlier cutoff — and a proper night's sleep, which is the thing caffeine has been quietly replacing.

Who Should Be Careful

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding — 200 mg cap; caffeine crosses the placenta.
  • Anxiety and panic disorders — caffeine amplifies the physical symptoms.
  • High blood pressure — a temporary spike after each dose; check with your doctor.
  • Heart rhythm conditions — palpitations are a common reason to reduce.
  • Acid reflux and IBS — caffeine relaxes the valve at the stomach and speeds the gut.
  • Poor sleep or insomnia — the first thing to change, before any supplement.
  • Medication interactions — some antibiotics, asthma drugs and decongestants interact; ask a pharmacist.
⚠️ Powdered caffeine is not the same as coffee. Pure caffeine powder is easy to overdose — a teaspoon is far more than a lethal dose for an adult. If you use it, weigh it in milligrams, never by spoon.

How to Cut Back Without Headaches

  1. Cut by a quarter every 3–4 days. Replace one coffee with tea, then a half-cup, then decaf. Slow enough that withdrawal stays mild.
  2. Protect the morning cup. Keep the one you actually enjoy; remove the ones you drink out of habit.
  3. Set a fixed cutoff time. 2pm is a reasonable start for most people; earlier if you're a slow metaboliser.
  4. Drink water first. Afternoon fatigue is often mild dehydration — see the water guide.
  5. Swap the last coffee for decaf or tea. Keeps the ritual, cuts the dose.
  6. Don't quit on a deadline. A taper over two weeks beats a heroic three-day crash you abandon on day four.
