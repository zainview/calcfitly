Heart Rate Zones Explained: What Are the 5 Zones?

Zone 2, fat burn, cardio, threshold — what these numbers on your smartwatch actually mean, and how to use them to train smarter, not just harder.

📅 Oct 4, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team
In this guide: What are heart rate zones? How to find your maximum heart rate The 5 zones, explained one by one Which zone burns the most fat? How to use zones in your training The Karvonen method (more accurate) FAQs

If you own a smartwatch or fitness band, you've seen them: Zone 1, Zone 2, Zone 3... little colored bars telling you how hard your heart is working. But what do these zones actually mean? And which one should you be in?

The good news: heart rate zones are simpler than they look. By the end of this guide, you'll know your personal zones, which one burns the most fat, and how to structure your workouts like people who actually know what they're doing.

What Are Heart Rate Zones?

Heart rate zones are simply ranges of heartbeats per minute, expressed as percentages of your maximum heart rate (the fastest your heart can safely beat). Each zone trains your body differently:

  • Lower zones = easier effort, more fat used as fuel, faster recovery
  • Higher zones = harder effort, more carbs used as fuel, more fitness gains per minute

Think of zones like gears on a bike. You wouldn't ride everywhere in the hardest gear — and you shouldn't exercise at maximum effort all the time either. Smart training means using the right gear for the right purpose.

How to Find Your Maximum Heart Rate

Everything starts with one number: your maximum heart rate (max HR). The classic formula has been around for decades:

Max Heart Rate Formula
Max HR = 220 − your age
Simple, fast, and accurate enough for most people.
📐 Quick Example
Age 30: 220 − 30 = 190 bpm max heart rate
Age 45: 220 − 45 = 175 bpm max heart rate
✅ All your zones are percentages of this number

Honest disclaimer: this formula is a population average. Your real max HR could be 10–15 beats higher or lower. That's normal — use it as a starting point and adjust based on how you actually feel.

The 5 Zones, Explained One by One

Zone % of Max HR Name How It Feels Main Benefit
Zone 1 50–60% Recovery Very easy, like a gentle walk Warm-up, cool-down, active recovery
Zone 2 60–70% Fat Burn / Base Easy — you can talk in full sentences Endurance base, fat metabolism
Zone 3 70–80% Cardio Moderate — short sentences only Cardiovascular fitness
Zone 4 80–90% Threshold Hard — a few words at a time Speed, lactate tolerance
Zone 5 90–100% Maximum All-out — can't talk at all Peak performance, sprints

Zone 1: Recovery (50–60%)

A 30-year-old's Zone 1 is roughly 95–114 bpm — a relaxed walk. Use it for warm-ups, cool-downs, and rest-day movement. It feels almost too easy, and that's the point.

Zone 2: The Famous Fat-Burning Zone (60–70%)

For our 30-year-old: 114–133 bpm. This is the zone endurance athletes live in. You can hold a full conversation, breathe comfortably, and keep going for a long time. Your body burns the highest percentage of fat here — more on that in a moment.

Zone 3: Cardio (70–80%)

At 133–152 bpm, you're working. Conversation becomes choppy. This zone builds cardiovascular fitness — a stronger heart and better oxygen delivery — but it's harder to sustain and recover from.

Zone 4: Threshold (80–90%)

152–171 bpm. This is "comfortably uncomfortable" — the pace of a fast 5K run. You can only hold it for limited periods, but it raises your speed and endurance ceiling like nothing else.

Zone 5: Maximum Effort (90–100%)

171–190 bpm. Sprint territory. Seconds to minutes at a time, not more. This zone builds peak power and is mostly for structured interval training — not everyday exercise.

Which Zone Burns the Most Fat? (The Truth)

Here's where most articles mislead you. Yes, Zone 2 is called the "fat-burning zone" — your body gets the highest percentage of its energy from fat there (around 50–65%). But percentage isn't the whole story.

Higher zones burn more total calories. And total calories matter more for weight loss than the fat percentage:

  • Zone 2 walk (30 min): ~150 kcal, about 60% from fat = 90 fat calories
  • Zone 4 run (30 min): ~350 kcal, about 35% from fat = 122 fat calories

See it? The harder workout burned more fat in absolute terms — plus far more total calories. And since safe weight loss comes from a calorie deficit, the "best" zone for losing weight is whichever one helps you move more, consistently.

The real magic of Zone 2: it's easy enough to do daily without exhaustion. Consistency beats intensity for most people.

How to Use Zones in Your Training (The 80/20 Rule)

Elite endurance athletes — runners, cyclists, rowers — follow a surprisingly simple structure called 80/20 training:

  • 80% of training time in Zones 1–2 — easy, conversational pace
  • 20% of training time in Zones 3–5 — moderate to hard

The mistake almost every beginner makes is the opposite: going too hard on easy days (stuck in Zone 3, the "gray zone") and too easy on hard days. Result? Always tired, rarely improving.

A simple week for a beginner might look like:

  1. 3–4 easy sessions (30–45 min Zone 2 — brisk walk, light jog, easy cycling)
  2. 1 harder session (Zone 4 intervals — 4 × 3 minutes hard, with rest between)
  3. 1–2 rest days (Zone 1 walking or full rest)

That's it. No complicated plan required.

The Karvonen Method (More Accurate Zones)

The 220-minus-age formula has one flaw: it treats everyone of the same age identically. A fit 30-year-old with a resting heart rate of 55 and an unfit one with 80 get the same zones — which makes little sense.

The Karvonen method fixes this by including your resting heart rate (measure it in the morning, before getting out of bed):

Karvonen Formula
Target HR = ((Max HR − Resting HR) × Intensity%) + Resting HR
Uses your heart rate reserve — the gap between resting and max.
📐 Example: Age 30, Resting HR 65, Zone 2 (60%)
Step 1: Max HR = 220 − 30 = 190
Step 2: Reserve = 190 − 65 = 125
Step 3: 125 × 0.60 = 75
Step 4: 75 + 65 = 140 bpm
✅ Zone 2 lower bound = 140 bpm (vs 114 with the basic method!)

Quite a difference, right? If you have a smartwatch and know your resting heart rate, the Karvonen method gives noticeably better numbers. Our heart rate zone calculator does both methods automatically.

⚠️ A quick note: If you have any heart condition, take beta-blockers or other heart medication, or feel chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath during exercise, talk to a doctor before training by heart rate. Medications can significantly change what your numbers mean.

Frequently Asked Questions
