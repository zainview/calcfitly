How Much Sugar Is Too Much? (Daily Limit + Hidden Sugar Chart)

One cola can already crosses a full day's limit. Here are the actual numbers — who recommends what, how much sugar hides in everyday food, and how to cut it in half without giving up everything you like.

📅 Nov 10, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ CalcFitly Team

Sugar advice is either "it's poison" or "it's just calories" — and both are unhelpful. The useful version has numbers in it.

The short answer: keep free sugars under 25 to 36 grams a day. That's about 6 to 9 teaspoons. The World Health Organization says under 10 percent of daily calories (roughly 50 g on a 2,000 calorie diet), and adds that under 5 percent — about 25 g — is better. The American Heart Association is stricter: 25 g for women, 36 g for men, 25 g for children.

Here's what those numbers look like in real food, why a single soft drink can cross the line, and where to cut first.

Daily Sugar Limits: The Numbers

Source Limit (free sugar) In teaspoons
WHO — upper limitUnder 10% of calories ≈ 50 g~12 tsp
WHO — idealUnder 5% of calories ≈ 25 g~6 tsp
AHA — womenMax 25 g~6 tsp
AHA — menMax 36 g~9 tsp
AHA — children (2–18)Max 25 g~6 tsp
UK NHS — adultsMax 30 g~7 tsp

Note the gap between "upper limit" and "ideal." Fifty grams is where risk clearly rises; twenty-five is where it flattens out. If you're choosing a target, 25 g is the one with the most agreement behind it.

Quick conversion
1 teaspoon ≈ 4 g of sugar
25 g ≈ 6 teaspoons · 36 g ≈ 9 teaspoons · a 330 ml cola ≈ 8¾ teaspoons

What Counts as "Free Sugar"

The limits only apply to free sugars — sugar added during manufacturing or cooking, plus the sugar in honey, syrups, fruit juice and juice concentrates. Naturally occurring sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk is not counted, because it comes packaged with fibre, water, protein or fat that change how fast it's absorbed.

So this counts:

  • Table sugar, jaggery, honey, maple syrup, glucose, dextrose, maltodextrin
  • Sugar in soft drinks, energy drinks, packaged juice and sweetened coffee
  • Fruit juice concentrate used to sweeten "no added sugar" products
  • Sugar in biscuits, cakes, cereal, flavoured yoghurt, sauces and dressings

And this doesn't:

  • Whole fruit — an apple has about 19 g of sugar, but it also has 4 g of fibre and a lot of water
  • Plain milk and curd — lactose, not added sugar
  • Vegetables, including sweet potato and carrot

The juice exception catches people out. A glass of orange juice has roughly the same sugar as a cola, minus the fibre, which is why it lands in the free-sugar column even though it started as fruit.

That's the real problem with sugar: it rarely arrives labelled as dessert. It comes in breakfast, drinks and "healthy" snacks.

Sugar, Calories and Fat Loss

Sugar doesn't bypass calorie balance. If you eat fewer calories than you burn, you lose fat whether or not sugar is in the plan. But sugar makes the deficit harder to hold, in four specific ways:

  • Liquid calories don't register. A 35 g cola is 140 calories that barely touch hunger. You drink it and eat the same lunch you would have eaten anyway.
  • It's calorie-dense and not filling. 500 calories of biscuits fit in a small plate; 500 calories of dal, rice and vegetables fills you for hours.
  • It triggers more eating. Sweet, low-fibre foods are easy to overeat, and the habit loops quickly — one biscuit becomes four.
  • It displaces protein and fibre. Every sweet snack is a missed chance at food that keeps you full.

So the practical move isn't "quit sugar" — it's "spend your sugar budget on things you actually enjoy, and stop spending it on drinks and snacks you don't even notice." If your weight has stalled, check your TDEE and your portions first; sugar is one lever, not the whole machine.

What Sugar Actually Does to Health

Away from weight, the evidence is strongest in a few areas:

  • Teeth. Frequency matters more than amount. Sipping something sweet all afternoon is worse for enamel than eating a dessert in one sitting.
  • Blood sugar and insulin. Large amounts of fast-digesting sugar, especially in drinks, make glucose control harder over time — particularly for people with insulin resistance.
  • Fatty liver. Fructose from sugary drinks is processed in the liver and, in excess, contributes to fat accumulation there.
  • Calories you don't notice. Which is the mechanism behind most of the weight gain attributed to sugar.

What sugar is not is uniquely fattening or addictive in the way headlines claim. Honey, jaggery and fruit sugar all behave similarly once digested. The dose and the delivery — liquid versus whole food — matter far more than the source.

Honey, Jaggery, Fruit: Same or Different?

Short version: swapping white sugar for honey or jaggery is a taste decision, not a health upgrade. Both sit in the same 25 g budget.

How to Read a Label in 5 Seconds

Ignore the front of the pack. Look at the per-100-gram column on the back:

Sugar per 100 g Verdict
22.5 g or moreHigh — treat as a treat, not a staple
5–22.5 gMedium — check the serving size you actually eat
5 g or lessLow — safe everyday option

Then scan the ingredients. Sugar hides under at least 50 names; the ones that matter most are sugar, cane sugar, dextrose, maltodextrin, corn syrup, glucose-fructose syrup, invert sugar, honey, jaggery, fruit juice concentrate and anything ending in "-ose." Ingredients are listed by weight, so if sugar appears in the first three, the product is mostly sugar by volume.

One trap: "no added sugar" means no added sugar — the product can still be full of naturally occurring or concentrate-based sugar. Check the number, not the claim.

7 Swaps That Halve Your Intake

Most people don't need to remove sugar, just move it. Each of these removes roughly 10–35 g with barely any lifestyle change:

  1. Swap one soft drink for water or soda with lemon. Saves 35 g instantly. This single change is usually the biggest win available.
  2. Eat the fruit, skip the juice. An orange has fibre and takes time; a glass of juice is 26 g of sugar in 30 seconds.
  3. Buy plain curd, add your own fruit. Flavoured cups carry 15–18 g; plain curd plus half a banana is about 7 g.
  4. Choose a cereal under 10 g sugar per 100 g. Plain oats, poha, eggs or upma all start near zero.
  5. Cut chai sugar from 2 spoons to 1, then to half. Taste adapts in about two weeks — this is 8 g a day saved, quietly.
  6. Keep sweets to a fixed time. One dessert after dinner beats grazing all day, both for teeth and for total intake.
  7. Read one label a week. Pick the product you eat most often and find its sugar number. That single habit changes more than any rule.

You don't have to hit 25 g tomorrow. Going from 80 g to 45 g is already a meaningful drop, and it's far more sustainable than a strict plan you quit in ten days.
