Understanding Your One Rep Max

The single number that powers every serious strength program.

🧮 What Is a 1RM and Why Estimate It?

Your one rep max is the heaviest weight you can lift once with good form. It's the gold standard for measuring strength — and nearly every structured program (5/3/1, Texas Method, most powerlifting plans) sets your working weights as percentages of it.

But here's the thing: actually testing a true 1RM is risky. Grinding out a maximal single rep stresses joints, demands perfect technique under fatigue, and requires an experienced spotter. That's why coaches estimate it instead — take a challenging set of 3–10 reps, plug it into a formula, and get a number that's typically within 2–5% of the real thing. Safer, faster, and accurate enough to program from.

📐 The 4 Formulas, Explained

FormulaEquationNotes
Epley (1985)weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)Most popular; simple and reliable
Brzycki (1993)weight × 36 ÷ (37 − reps)Very close to Epley under 10 reps
Lander (1985)(100 × weight) ÷ (101.3 − 2.67 × reps)Slightly more conservative
Lombardi (1989)weight × reps^0.10Handles higher rep sets better

This calculator shows all four and uses their average as your estimate — smoothing out each formula's individual quirks.

🎯 How to Use Your Percentage Chart

Your results include a chart of weights at each percentage of your 1RM. Here's how to train with it:

  • Pure strength (85–95%, 1–5 reps): Heavy sets with long rests (3–5 min). Builds maximal force and neural efficiency.
  • Muscle growth (65–85%, 6–12 reps): The classic hypertrophy range. Moderate rests (1–2 min), higher total volume.
  • Endurance (50–65%, 12–20+ reps): Lighter sets for work capacity, technique practice, and joint-friendly volume.

Most good programs spend the majority of time in the 65–85% range, with occasional heavy and light weeks mixed in.

✅ Getting an Accurate Estimate

  • Use sets of 3–5 reps: The sweet spot for accuracy. Singles are risky; sets above 10 reps measure endurance more than strength and skew formulas high.
  • Take the set close to failure: Stop with 0–1 reps left in the tank. A casual, easy set of 5 tells the formula nothing.
  • Use strict form: Bounced reps, half squats, and spotter-assisted reps inflate your estimate — and inflate your training weights beyond what you can handle.
  • Estimate per lift: Your bench 1RM formula result doesn't apply to your squat. Calculate each lift separately.
  • Recalculate regularly: Every time your working sets improve, your estimated 1RM improves. Update every 4–6 weeks.

⚠️ If You Do Test a True 1RM

Experienced lifters sometimes want the real number. Do it safely:

  • Warm up progressively: Empty bar → 50% × 5 → 70% × 3 → 80% × 2 → 90% × 1, then attempt your max.
  • Always use a spotter or safety arms — especially on bench press and squat. No exceptions.
  • Jump in small steps: Add 2.5–5 kg per attempt, not 10–20 kg.
  • Limit attempts: 3–5 maximal tries per session, with 3–5 minutes rest between them.
  • Skip it if you're beat up: Poor sleep, soreness, or a nagging joint means test another day. The estimate will still be there.
FAQs

1RM Questions, Answered

Quick answers to common strength questions.

What is a one rep max (1RM)?

Your one rep max is the maximum weight you can lift for a single repetition with proper form. It's the standard measure of strength and the basis for percentage-based training programs.

How do I calculate my 1RM without testing it?

Use a submaximal set: lift a weight you can manage for 3–10 reps to near failure, then apply a formula like Epley (weight × (1 + reps/30)). Sets of 3–5 reps give the most accurate estimates.

Which 1RM formula is most accurate?

No single formula wins for everyone. Epley and Brzycki are the most popular and tend to be within a few percent of each other for sets under 10 reps. This calculator averages four formulas for a balanced estimate.

Is it safe to test my true 1RM?

True 1RM testing carries injury risk, especially for beginners. If you're new to lifting (under a year of training), stick with calculated estimates. Experienced lifters should test with a spotter, proper warm-up, and small weight jumps.

What percentage of my 1RM should I train with?

It depends on your goal: strength (85–95% for 1–5 reps), muscle growth (65–85% for 6–12 reps), and muscular endurance (50–65% for 12–20+ reps). Most programs rotate through all three ranges.

How often should I retest my 1RM?

Every 6–12 weeks is plenty. Strength changes slowly, and frequent max testing interferes with training. A better habit: recalculate from your working sets every few weeks — if your 5-rep set goes up, your 1RM went up too.
