Understanding Protein

The building block of muscle — and the most important macro to get right.

🧮 How Much Protein Do You Really Need?

Your protein needs depend on two things: how active you are and what your goal is. Here's what the research supports:

  • Sedentary adults: 0.8–1.0 g per kg — the official minimum to prevent deficiency.
  • Active people (maintenance): 1.2–1.6 g per kg — supports recovery and general health.
  • Fat loss: 1.8–2.2 g per kg — higher protein protects muscle while you're in a calorie deficit and keeps you fuller for longer.
  • Muscle building: 1.6–2.2 g per kg — the range research consistently shows maximizes muscle growth with resistance training.

Notice something? Whether cutting or bulking, the target barely changes. High protein is almost always the right answer.

⏰ Does Protein Timing Matter?

Total daily intake matters far more than timing — but a few simple habits help:

  • Spread it out: 3–5 meals with 20–40 g of protein each maximizes muscle protein synthesis better than one huge serving.
  • Include protein at breakfast: Most people eat very little protein before noon. Starting strong makes the daily target much easier.
  • Post-workout window: The "30-minute anabolic window" is largely a myth — but having protein within a couple of hours of training is a sensible habit.
  • Before bed: A slow-digesting protein (like Greek yogurt or paneer) supports overnight recovery.

🌱 Protein for Vegetarians and Vegans

Hitting your target on a plant-based diet is absolutely possible — it just takes a bit more planning:

  • Combine sources: Dal with rice, hummus with whole wheat — combining legumes and grains creates complete amino acid profiles.
  • Aim slightly higher: Plant proteins are less digestible, so add 10–20% to your target.
  • Lean on the stars: Tofu, tempeh, seitan, paneer, Greek yogurt, lentils, chickpeas, and soy milk are your best friends.
  • Consider a plant protein powder: Pea or soy protein makes hitting high targets much easier.

⚠️ Common Protein Myths

  • "High protein damages kidneys": Not in healthy people. Studies show intakes up to 2.5–3 g per kg are safe for those with normal kidney function. Existing kidney disease is the exception — consult a doctor.
  • "You can only absorb 30 g per meal": A myth. Your body absorbs nearly all the protein you eat — it just uses it at different rates. Spreading intake is still smart for muscle building, but nothing is "wasted."
  • "Protein powder is necessary": No. It's convenient, not magic. Whole foods can cover your needs — powder is just an easy top-up.
  • "More protein = more muscle": Only up to a point. Beyond ~2.2 g per kg, extra protein provides little additional benefit. Training hard matters more.
FAQs

Protein Questions, Answered

Quick answers to common protein questions.

How much protein do I need per day?

For sedentary adults, the minimum is 0.8 g per kg of body weight. For active people and muscle building, research supports 1.6–2.2 g per kg. A 70 kg person training regularly needs roughly 112–154 g daily.

How much protein should I eat to build muscle?

Research consistently shows 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight daily maximizes muscle growth when combined with resistance training. More than this provides little extra benefit for most people.

Can you eat too much protein?

For healthy people, high protein intakes (up to 2.5–3 g per kg) appear safe in studies. People with existing kidney disease should consult a doctor first. The main downside of excess protein is simply that it crowds out other nutrients.

How much protein can the body absorb per meal?

The idea that you can only absorb 30 g per meal is a myth — your body absorbs nearly all protein you eat. However, for optimal muscle building, spreading intake across 3–5 meals of 20–40 g each works better than one large serving.

What are the best sources of protein?

Top sources include chicken breast (31 g per 100 g), eggs (6 g each), Greek yogurt (10 g per 100 g), fish, paneer, tofu, lentils, and whey protein powder. Mixing animal and plant sources covers all essential amino acids.

Do vegetarians need more protein?

Slightly. Plant proteins are a bit less digestible, so vegetarians and vegans may benefit from aiming 10–20% higher and combining sources (like dal with rice) to get complete amino acid profiles.
